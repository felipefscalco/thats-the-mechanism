## #001 — QR code (Tech) · renders/QR.mp4

**Title (YT Shorts, ≤60 chars):** Why a logo doesn't break a QR code
**Alt titles:** A QR code carries its own spare parts · Cover this corner and the QR code dies

**Description:**
A QR code is built to lose part of itself. When it's generated, your link is split into bytes and the encoder adds Reed–Solomon repair bytes — the same math that kept scratched CDs playing. At level H a scanner can restore about 30% of the code's bytes, which is why a centred logo works. Real-world tests put the safe logo size around 15% of the area, because glare and scratches spend the same budget. Cover one of the three corner "finder" squares, though, and nothing scans — those aren't data, they're how the camera finds the code.

The QR in this video is real (level H) — point your camera at it.

## #002 — Elevator (Engineering) · renders/Elevator.mp4

**Title:** An elevator doesn't lift you
**Alt titles:** Why an empty elevator would rise · An elevator is a scale, not a crane

**Description:**
Traction elevators hang a counterweight on the other side of the cable, sized to the empty car plus about 40–50% of the rated load (the "overbalance"). The motor only fights the difference: with a full car it helps the car up; with a near-empty car the counterweight is heavier and the motor holds it back. That's why an empty car, with the brake released, would rise instead of fall — and why a modest motor can move a full office all day.

Sources:
• TK Elevator — Counterweights: https://www.tkelevator.com/us-en/tools/classroom-on-demand/counterweights.html

## #003 — Blue sky & red sunset (Physics) · renders/Sky.mp4

**Title:** The blue sky and the red sunset are the same trick
**Alt titles:** Your sunset is someone else's blue sky · Why sunsets are red (it's the sky's fault)

**Description:**
Air molecules are far smaller than a light wave, and small particles scatter short wavelengths much more than long ones (Rayleigh scattering, ∝ 1/λ⁴): blue around 450 nm scatters roughly four times more than red around 650 nm. At noon you see that scattered blue from every direction — that's the sky. At sunset the light reaching you crosses dozens of times more air, the blue is scattered out of the beam along the way (into someone else's afternoon sky), and what's left is orange and red.

Atmosphere thickness in the diagram is exaggerated so it fits on a phone.
